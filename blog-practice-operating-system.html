The short answer. A direction, not a product.

Over time, many advice practices are likely to move from a collection of separate tools towards a more connected way of working, sometimes described as a practice operating system. In that picture, client context, advice work, approvals and evidence sit together, AI prepares the work and people make the decisions. It is a direction, not a product you buy in one step. The practical question today is which part of the journey to connect first.

Where most practices are today. Context, reconstructed.

A client relationship crosses more than a contact record. There are enquiries, meetings, file notes, fact finds, SoA requests, approvals, implementation and reviews. In many firms these sit across CRM or advice software, document storage, email, calendars, specialist modelling and research tools, and investment platforms.

When the links between these activities depend on someone remembering what happened, the team spends time reconstructing context. The result is often a pile of prework between the meeting and the advice. Map one client journey before choosing software: note where information starts, who needs it next and what tells them they can proceed.

Where things may be heading. A connected practice.

Looking further ahead, the direction is towards a connected practice. A fact find, a file note, an SoA request and a review become steps in one record of the relationship rather than separate files in separate places. Handovers are visible, and anyone authorised can see what is waiting and why.

This is a long-term view of where the industry may go, not a description of any single product available today, including ours. Specialist modelling and research tools, and investment platforms, are likely to remain specialist for some time, and the connections between them will continue to need assessment.

AI prepares. People decide.

AI can help prepare notes, drafts and agreed actions using the relevant context. It works best as part of a clear process: establish the inputs, describe the intended output and identify who reviews it.

Human approval is a gate, not a formality. A clickthrough is not advice approval, and advisers keep strategy and product judgement. A reusable skill can organise the preparation, but does not make every output correct. Exception handling is part of the design, particularly when information is incomplete.

Why it happens in steps. Start where advice waits.

Few practices will rebuild everything at once. A sensible path starts with the workflow that is most backed up and connects more as the practice grows. For many firms that is the work between the first meeting and the Statement of Advice.

A connected system still needs ownership of the day-to-day operation. Agree who manages access, maintains integrations, resolves exceptions and approves changes. Compare the implementation and ongoing responsibilities together.

A worked example. One new client, end to end.

Consider a prospective client who books a first meeting. This is an illustrative workflow, not a customer result or a promise that every step or integration is included by default.

  1. Hold the first meeting. Using the firm’s agreed capture process, a draft file note is prepared. The adviser checks it against their own recollection before it becomes the record.
  2. Prepare the fact find. AI-assisted preparation organises the information the client has supplied into fact-find inputs. Gaps and conflicts are flagged for a person to resolve, not filled with assumptions.
  3. Prepare the SoA request. AI prepares a draft SoA request pack for the paraplanner. The paraplanner verifies it and the adviser confirms the strategy before work proceeds.
  4. Approve and sign off. The adviser makes the advice decisions. The approval is kept with the client record.
  5. Carry the work through to implementation. Each agreed action has an owner and a place in the client workflow. The team can see what is outstanding without reconstructing the conversation.

The useful connection is the handover: each person knows what they have received, what they need to check and what happens next.

What to ask of software today. Compare the process, not the category.

Well-configured CRM or advice software may already support workflows and integrations. Compare the actual process in your firm rather than assuming that a product category tells you what it can do.

Questions to ask when evaluating a connected workflow
AreaWhat to check
Client contextCan authorised staff find the relevant records, documents and conversation history?
HandoverIs the next action assigned, with clear completion criteria and a route for exceptions?
AI preparationAre the inputs, output, reviewer and permitted actions defined?
ApprovalIs it clear who approved each step, and what a draft cannot do until then?
ConnectionsWhich steps are connected automatically, and which require a person to transfer or confirm information?
OwnershipWhat components, access and documentation are delivered, and what ongoing dependencies remain?

A checklist for your next demo. Bring a real process.

Bring a real process, using sample or appropriately prepared information. Ask the provider to show the following:

  • A complete journey, including the handover between two team members.
  • What happens when a required document is missing or the client match is uncertain.
  • What different roles can see and change.
  • How an AI-generated draft is checked, rejected or corrected.
  • The distinction between features shown in the demo and those included in your proposed build.
  • The migration, training, licensing and support responsibilities after go-live.

Record the answers against your requirements. A clear scope is more useful than an impressive demonstration with unanswered questions about daily use.
